How the forecast is built

Every Tachyon Flux probability comes from a stack of statistical models that never see a bookmaker price. Rating engines estimate team strength, a Bayesian layer learns which factors matter and how unsure we are, a simulator plays each match 1,000,000 times, and two machine-learning models give independent second opinions.

1. Rating engines: how strong is each team?

Three engines estimate each team’s expected goals for the match. All are re-fitted walk-forward, so a rating before a match never contains that match.

Dixon–Coles (1997)

Goals are modelled as Poisson counts driven by attack strength, defence strength and a home advantage per league:

\log \lambda_{home} = h_{league} + \alpha_{home} + \delta_{away}, \qquad \log \mu_{away} = \alpha_{away} + \delta_{home}

A plain Poisson model gets low scores wrong: real football has more 0-0 and 1-1 draws than independent Poisson counts predict. Dixon–Coles corrects the four low-score cells with a dependence parameter ρ, which we fit from data rather than fixing it:

P(x, y) = \tau_{\rho}(x, y; \lambda, \mu)\; \mathrm{Pois}(x; \lambda)\; \mathrm{Pois}(y; \mu)

  • Time decay: each past match is weighted by e^(−ξ · days ago), with ξ = 0.0019 per day (a half-life of about one year), so recent form counts more.
  • One scale per country: all divisions of a country are fitted together, so promoted and relegated teams carry their rating across leagues.
  • Ridge penalty: teams with few matches are pulled towards average instead of producing extreme ratings.
  • Fitting: weighted maximum likelihood with analytic gradients, warm-started from the previous week’s fit.

xG-Dixon–Coles

The same model fitted on “performance goals”: half the actual score plus half the chance quality (expected goals where available, otherwise shots on target × league conversion rate). Chance quality is steadier than goals, so this engine reacts less to lucky or unlucky results.

Kalman filter: ratings that move

The Kalman filter treats every team’s attack and defence as a hidden state that drifts week to week: a state-space model with a Gaussian state and a Poisson observation.

  • Predict: before each match, the uncertainty of a team’s rating grows with the days since its last game, and grows extra after the summer break.
  • Update: after each match, the rating moves by an amount that depends on how surprising the result was and how sure we already were (a Newton/Laplace step on the Poisson likelihood).
  • Observation: 0.5 × goals + 0.5 × chance quality, so it learns from performance, not just scorelines.

ClubElo

An independent Elo rating for every European club on one scale, taken from the Monday before each match, so a Norwegian side can be compared with an English one.

2. Bayesian layer: what matters, and how sure are we?

The MCMC layer learns how much everything else matters (rest days, form, line-up strength, Elo gaps and other pre-match features) and attaches an honest uncertainty to every forecast.

\log E[\text{goals}_s] = w_{dc}\log\lambda^{dc}_s + w_{xg}\log\lambda^{xg}_s + w_{k}\log\lambda^{k}_s + \sum_j \beta_j\, x_{sj}, \qquad \beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.15^2)

The prior is deliberately sceptical: every effect starts at zero and must earn its size from the data. Our own Metropolis–Hastings sampler starts at the posterior mode with a Hessian-shaped (Laplace) proposal and mixes in seconds. Each match gets 300 posterior draws of both teams’ expected goals, which feed the 90% ranges shown next to each probability.

Calibration: two parameters (sharpness a and a draw shift b) are fitted on a later time window, on predictions the model never trained on, so that when Tachyon Flux says 70%, it happens about 70% of the time.

p_i' \propto p_i^{\,a}\; e^{\,b\,[i = \text{draw}]}

3. Monte Carlo simulator: a million matches, minute by minute

r_t = \bar{r} \cdot m(t) \cdot g(\text{state}) \cdot c(\text{red cards}) \cdot e^{W_t} \cdot \text{shocks} \cdot \text{referee}

Every factor is measured from 32,303 real matches with full event data, except the chaos terms, which are stated assumptions.

Factor What it captures Value
Minute profile m(t) Goals cluster late in each half Share of goals per minute, 1–95
Game state g Teams change strategy with the score, as game theory predicts Level ×1.09, leading ×0.96, trailing ×0.92 (measured)
Red cards c A sending-off shifts the balance 0.10% per team-minute; 10 men ×0.54, opponent ×1.56
Penalties Scaled by each referee’s tendency 8% of all goals
Momentum Do teams score more right after scoring? No: −7%, so the model does not invent momentum
Brownian drift e^(W_t) Fatigue, confidence and tactical shifts move the scoring rate Random walk, σ = 0.015 per minute (assumed)
Shocks Injuries, tactical collapse 0.2% per minute, rate ×0.85 (assumed)

Brownian motion: in 1827 Robert Brown watched pollen grains jitter in water; Einstein later explained it as countless molecular collisions. A match behaves the same way, so W_t is a Brownian random walk in each team’s scoring rate, and no two simulated matches unfold alike.

MCMC inside the Monte Carlo: the million simulations are split across the 300 posterior draws, so each forecast carries both the randomness of football and the model’s own uncertainty about team strength.

4. Second opinions

Vector database: each match is stored as a 16-number embedding (PCA of ratings and pre-match features). For a new match we find the 200 closest past matches by cosine similarity and count how they ended.

Transformer: each team’s last 12 matches are read as a sequence of tokens; self-attention decides which past matches matter most, and the network builds a 64-dimensional picture of each team. Trained before July 2023 and judged once on 2024/25.

5. Consensus, risk penalty and score

Consensus is the average of the available layers (model, vector, transformer and the market benchmark with the bookmaker margin removed). The risk penalty takes points off for warning signs in team news and probable line-ups. Score = consensus − penalty; picks are ranked by it.

6. How we keep it honest

  • Leak-free: every rating, feature and fit uses only matches played strictly before the one being forecast.
  • Separate windows: fitted on 2023/24, judged once on 2024/25; the first half of 2026 stays sealed as a final test.
  • Pre-registered layers: each idea is written down before testing and kept only if it beats the current model on the same matches.
  • Tested and not used: bivariate Poisson, league-table stakes, weather, and blending the vector and transformer layers into the main model.
